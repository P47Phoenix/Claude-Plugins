# Genesis 2:18–24 — "The Institution of Marriage"

## 1. Original Context

**Historical/literary setting:** Genesis 2 is the second creation account, focusing in on humanity after the wide-lens account of Genesis 1. It's part of the Torah's opening narrative, written to a Hebrew audience for whom marriage, family lines, and covenant were central to identity. The passage sits right after God forms the man (Gen 2:7) and places him in the garden with a task (2:15) — marriage here is introduced as part of the created order, not an afterthought.

**Audience:** Originally addressed to Israel, for whom this text grounded marriage as a divine institution rather than a human social convention — distinguishing Israelite thought from surrounding Ancient Near Eastern cultures where marriage customs varied widely and were often framed in more transactional or mythological terms.

**Structure:** God declares a problem (2:18), demonstrates it through the naming of animals (2:19–20), then resolves it by forming the woman from the man (2:21–22), followed by the man's response (2:23) and a narrator's summary statement (2:24).

## 2. Original-Language Notes

- **"Not good" (lo-tov, לֹא־טוֹב) — v.18.** Striking because everything else in Genesis 1 is called "good" or "very good." This is the first "not good" in Scripture — signaling that aloneness, not sin, is the deficiency being addressed.
- **"Helper" (ezer, עֵזֶר) — v.18, 20.** This word is often misunderstood in English as implying subordination. In the Hebrew Bible, ezer is used most frequently of God himself as Israel's helper (e.g. Psalm 121:2, "my help comes from the LORD"). It denotes a vital, often stronger party coming to aid another — not a subordinate assistant. Paired with "fit for him" / "corresponding to him" (kenegdo, כְּנֶגְדּוֹ) — literally "as in front of him" or "corresponding to him" — the phrase describes a counterpart, not a lesser.
- **"Bone of my bones, flesh of my flesh" (v.23)** — a Hebrew idiom for kinship and shared identity (used elsewhere, e.g. Genesis 29:14, of family belonging), not merely poetic flourish.
- **"Woman" (ishah, אִשָּׁה) / "Man" (ish, אִישׁ) (v.23)** — a wordplay in Hebrew (ish/ishah) that doesn't fully carry into English; the naming reflects shared origin and nature.
- **"One flesh" (basar echad, בָּשָׂר אֶחָד) (v.24)** — denotes a unity that is more than physical union; it signals a new, singular kinship unit replacing the prior primary bonds (leaving father and mother).

## 3. Theological Significance

- Marriage is instituted by God, prior to the fall — meaning it belongs to creation's good design, not merely to redemption's fix for sin.
- The woman is described as a corresponding, powerful counterpart, not a subordinate — a corrective to any reading of headship as inherent superiority (a theme Ephesians 5 later builds directly on).
- "One flesh" establishes marriage as a covenantal, unifying bond that Jesus himself cites as authoritative and binding (Matthew 19:4–6, quoting Genesis 2:24).
- This passage is the Old Testament root that Paul draws on directly in Ephesians 5:31, tying the "one flesh" language to the Christ-and-church relationship — making Genesis 2 the foundation the rest of the "loving your wife as Christ loved the church" sermon builds on.

## 4. Suggested Homiletical Structure

**I. The Problem God Names (v.18)**
Aloneness is declared "not good" — even in an unfallen world. Connect to human design for relationship, not self-sufficiency.

**II. The Search That Comes Up Empty (v.19–20)**
Adam names the animals — establishing dominion — but finds no counterpart. Use this to illustrate: not every relationship satisfies the deep need for a true counterpart; something uniquely fitted was still missing.

**III. The Provision God Makes (v.21–23)**
God doesn't simply speak the woman into existence separately — he forms her from the man himself, then brings her to him. Emphasize shared origin, equal dignity ("bone of my bones"), and the language of ezer kenegdo — a strong, corresponding helper.

**IV. The Pattern Set for All Marriage (v.24)**
"Leave and cleave" — a new primary loyalty is formed. One flesh. This becomes the pattern Jesus and Paul both return to when speaking of marriage's seriousness and design.

**V. Forward Pointer**
Close by naming (without fully developing, since that's a different message) that Paul takes this exact "one flesh" language in Ephesians 5:31–32 and applies it to Christ and the church — marriage was designed from the beginning to be a living picture of that greater covenant love.

- **Primary text:** Genesis 2:18–24
- **Cross-references:** Matthew 19:4–6 (Jesus citing Genesis 2:24 as authoritative); Ephesians 5:31–32 (Paul applying "one flesh" to Christ and the church); Psalm 121:2 (parallel use of ezer for God as helper); Genesis 29:14 (parallel use of "bone of my bones" idiom for kinship)
