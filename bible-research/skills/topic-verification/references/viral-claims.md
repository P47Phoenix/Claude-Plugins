# Viral Claims: Pattern Library

Recurring folk claims that spread through sermons, memes and social posts. Use this to **recognize** a claim and check it against real sources. Each entry: the claim, what real sources say, and the citation.

**Rule:** only entries with a source you located and opened are written as findings. Where no real source was found, the entry is marked `[needs source]` and the claim must be reported as **unverifiable**, not as false and not as true. The list of open items is in `DESIGN_NOTES.md`. Source notes say what was opened; check the original before quoting in a report.

Confidence labels: **high** (source opened and relevant), **medium** (reported by a secondary source), **needs source** (nothing located).

## 1. "Abba means Daddy"

- **Claim:** The Aramaic *abba* (Mark 14:36; Romans 8:15; Galatians 4:6) is a child's word, best translated "Daddy"
- **What real sources say:** James Barr argued that *abba* was an ordinary adult and family address meaning "father," not a baby word, and that the evidence supports "father" over "daddy." The "daddy" reading is traced to Joachim Jeremias, who described it as a child's word (1971). The point of the passages is the intimacy and authority of addressing God as Father, not a baby-talk nuance
- **Citation:** James Barr, "Abba Isn't Daddy," *Journal of Theological Studies* 39.1 (April 1988), pp. 28-47, doi:10.1093/jts/39.1.28. Summary of Barr's argument and the link to Jeremias via Glenn T. Stanton, "FactChecker: Does Abba Mean Daddy?", The Gospel Coalition, 13 May 2013. Jeremias: *New Testament Theology* (1971), page not verified
- **Confidence:** high for the Barr citation; medium for the content (read Barr directly before quoting it)

## 2. The "eye of the needle" was a small gate in Jerusalem

- **Claim:** The "eye of a needle" (Matthew 19:24; Mark 10:25; Luke 18:25) was a low city gate a camel could pass only by kneeling and unloading
- **What real sources say:** Most scholars reject the gate reading. A study of the origin finds that Theophylact, often credited with the idea, says nothing about a gate on this verse, and that the earliest known mention is a gloss attributed to Anselm of Canterbury (11th century) preserved in Aquinas's *Catena aurea*. The saying is best read as deliberate hyperbole
- **Citation:** Agnieszka Ziemińska, "The Origin of the 'Needle's Eye Gate' Myth: Theophylact or Anselm?", *New Testament Studies* 68.3 (2022), pp. 358-361, doi:10.1017/S0028688521000448
- **Confidence:** high (the Anselm attribution is hedged in the article itself)

## 3. John 14:2-3 as the "Jewish wedding custom"

- **Claim:** A Jewish groom went away to build a room onto his father's house, then returned to take his bride; Jesus is using that custom in John 14:2-3
- **What real sources say:** `[needs source]`. A search found the custom stated in sermons and devotionals with no primary source. One page pointed to Mishnah Ketubot 5:2 and the Jerusalem Talmud, but this was only seen in a search snippet and was not verified. No scholarly source was opened
- **Report as:** **unverifiable**. Do not report it as false or true. If a draft or a post uses it, remove or flag it
- **Next step:** read the commentaries on John 14:2-3 directly (for example Raymond Brown, *The Gospel According to John*, Anchor Bible 29A, and Craig Keener's commentary on John) and check Mishnah Ketubot and the Talmudic discussion of betrothal and marriage on Sefaria. Do not cite either work for this point until it has been opened
- **Confidence:** needs source

## 4. Agape is "God-love" and phileo is "brotherly love"

- **Claim:** In John 21:15-17, Jesus uses *agapao* (a higher, divine love) and Peter answers with *phileo* (a lesser, human love); the difference shows Peter's falling short
- **What real sources say:** D. A. Carson treats this as a lexical fallacy. The two verbs overlap substantially in this period of Greek, so a special sense cannot be read into the verb choice. He criticizes the argument of William Hendriksen. Secondary sources report that he cites the Septuagint's use of *agapao* in 2 Samuel 13:15, where it describes Amnon's lust, as evidence that the word is not limited to "divine" love
- **Citation:** D. A. Carson, *Exegetical Fallacies* (Baker Academic; the opened review describes the 2002 printing; confirm edition), the chapter on word-study fallacies. As reported by Tony Garland's review at spiritandtruth.org. **Page number for the John 21 discussion: not verified; secondary sources conflict.** Leave the page blank or check the book
- **Lexicon:** BDAG s.v. *agapao* and *agape*: `[needs source]` (not opened)
- **Confidence:** medium

## 5. "Fear not" appears 365 times

- **Claim:** The Bible says "do not be afraid" 365 times, once for each day of the year
- **What real sources say:** No standard translation contains 365 occurrences. One count reports the phrase and its close equivalents at fewer than 100 times in the NIV, about 80 in the ESV, and just over 70 in the NKJV. The count varies with which phrases are counted, and the 365 figure has no named origin; it spread through sermons and social media
- **Citation:** John Daniels, "Does 'Be Not Afraid' Really Appear 365 Times in the Bible?", Theology In Five (Substack), 15 October 2025. **Popular source only**; not scholarly. Confirm counts with a concordance of the translation in use before quoting any number
- **Confidence:** medium for the conclusion; low for authority

## 6. *Tetelestai* means "paid in full" (from ancient receipts)

- **Claim:** Jesus' cry in John 19:30 uses a Greek word stamped on ancient receipts and tax records meaning "paid in full," so it is a statement about debt paid
- **What real sources say:** The reading entered scholarship through Moulton and Milligan's *Vocabulary of the Greek Testament* (1915), based on Egyptian papyrus receipts published from 1896. A later examination of about 315 tariff receipts found that nearly all use the abbreviation τετελ, read since 1934 as τετελώνηται ("paid as taxes"). The receipts record cargo taxes, not debts, and the scholar found neither that abbreviation nor *tetelestai* on ordinary purchase or debt receipts. Elsewhere the verb means that a task is completed; he prefers "it is finished," with Scripture fulfilled. A different writer noted one receipt that does use *tetelestai* itself, so the point is **contested**
- **Citation:** Gary Manning Jr., "Paid in Full? The Meaning of Tetelestai in Jesus' Final Words," Biola Good Book Blog, 20 April 2022. **Blog post, not peer-reviewed.** Moulton and Milligan, *The Vocabulary of the Greek Testament* (1915): origin of the claim, not opened. Check BDAG s.v. *teleo* before quoting
- **Confidence:** high that Moulton and Milligan is the origin and that Manning critiques it; medium for the details

## Adding an Entry

1. Locate and open a real source (a lexicon entry, a journal article, a standard commentary)
2. Write the claim, what the source says (paraphrased, no long quotation), and the full citation
3. Record what was opened and what was reported by a secondary source
4. If no source can be found, write the entry with `[needs source]` and add it to the open-items list in `DESIGN_NOTES.md`
5. Never mark an entry "false" without a source; use **unverifiable** if the claim has no support but you also cannot show it is wrong
