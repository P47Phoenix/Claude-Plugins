# Song Theology: Common Fault Lines

Doctrinal fault lines that recur in hymns and worship songs. Use this to **label** a line accurately. Almost every item here is **contested across traditions**, not wrong. A line is called wrong (textually unsupported) only when it contradicts the biblical text itself. Songs are poetry; apply the poetic-license test in `claim-extraction.md` first.

## How to Use

1. Find the doctrinal assumption in the line (not the words; positions only, guardrail 9)
2. Match it to a fault line below
3. Label it: **fits one tradition, contested in another**, or **textually unsupported**
4. Name the traditions and the scripture each reading rests on
5. Say what is fine to sing and what to be careful about

Never reproduce the lyric. Cite title, songwriter and line position.

## Fault Lines

### 1. Atonement: penal substitution vs. Christus Victor (and others)

- **What it is:** What Jesus' death accomplished. **Penal substitution** (Christ took the penalty for sin in our place) and **Christus Victor** (Christ defeated the powers of sin, death and the devil) are two major pictures. Others include ransom, satisfaction and moral influence
- **Scripture:** Penal substitution leans on Isaiah 53:5-6, 2 Corinthians 5:21, Galatians 3:13 and Romans 3:25. Christus Victor leans on Colossians 2:15, Hebrews 2:14 and 1 John 3:8
- **Label:** Both have scriptural footing and long histories. A song that holds only one is **fitting one tradition**, not wrong. It becomes a concern only if it denies the other as unbiblical, or if it asserts something neither supports
- **Reference to confirm before citing:** Gustaf Aulén, *Christus Victor* (1931) names the "classic" view; check the edition and page before quoting

### 2. "God's wrath satisfied": propitiation vs. expiation

- **What it is:** Whether Christ's death turns away God's wrath (**propitiation**) or only removes sin (**expiation**). The Greek word *hilasterion* / *hilasmos* (Romans 3:25; 1 John 2:2; 4:10) is the center of the debate
- **Label:** **Contested across traditions and among translators.** Many English versions render it "propitiation" and others "atoning sacrifice" or "expiation." Say which a song assumes; do not call the other wrong
- **Lexicon:** check BDAG s.v. *hilasmos* and *hilasterion*. The classic debate is between C. H. Dodd (expiation) and Leon Morris (propitiation); confirm exact titles and pages before citing either
- **Related line type:** "the Father poured his wrath on the Son." Many traditions affirm the idea in some form; some reject the wording as a break between Father and Son (see fault line 3)

### 3. "The Father turned his back on the Son"

- **What it is:** A common phrasing of Jesus' cry in Matthew 27:46 and Mark 15:34. Jesus is quoting Psalm 22:1. Some traditions read it as real abandonment in judgment; others read it as the opening of a psalm that ends in vindication and as a cry from within the unbroken unity of the Trinity
- **Scripture:** Psalm 22:1 and 22:24 ("he has not hidden his face"); Matthew 27:46; John 16:32
- **Label:** **Contested across traditions.** A statement that the Father and Son were divided is **textually weak** and may collide with the unity of God stated in the Nicene Creed. Name both readings; do not call either heresy without reason

### 4. Trinity-collapsing pronouns (modalism)

- **What it is:** Lines that blur the Persons, for example addressing the Father as the one who died, or ending a prayer to the Father by naming the Spirit as if one Person were another. **Modalism** (one God who appears in three modes, not three Persons) is rejected in the Nicene and Athanasian creeds
- **Scripture:** Matthew 3:16-17; John 14:16-17; 2 Corinthians 13:14
- **Label:** Ordinary poetic shorthand ("God" for any Person) is **not error**. Flag only when the line affirms that the Father or Spirit suffered or died, or denies the distinction of Persons. Where it is ambiguous, mark it **ambiguous** and suggest a clarifying edit

### 5. The Holy Spirit and spiritual gifts

- **What it is:** Language about the Spirit "falling", being "poured out" or "moving" in a room. Traditions differ on whether the gifts of the Spirit continue (**continuationist**) or ended (**cessationist**), and on how the Spirit is experienced
- **Scripture:** Acts 2; 1 Corinthians 12-14; Ephesians 5:18
- **Label:** **Contested across traditions.** Treat such language as poetry unless it makes a specific claim (for example, a promise of a particular manifestation)

### 6. "I am no longer a sinner" and related identity language

- **What it is:** Lines that state the believer's new identity in strong terms. The Lutheran and Reformed idea *simul justus et peccator* ("at once righteous and sinner") and the Wesleyan idea of entire sanctification read these texts differently
- **Scripture:** Romans 6-8; 1 John 1:8-10; 2 Corinthians 5:17; Galatians 2:20
- **Label:** **Contested across traditions.** A line that denies any ongoing sin faces 1 John 1:8; flag it as in tension with that verse. A line about identity in Christ is not error

### 7. Promises lifted from their context

- **What it is:** A verse given to a singer as a personal promise when it was addressed to Israel or to a church. Common examples are exile promises and military promises
- **Scripture to check:** the verse in its paragraph, for example Jeremiah 29:4-14 for the "plans" verse
- **Label:** **Textually unsupported** where the song claims the promise applies unchanged to the singer and the context says otherwise. This is a context issue, not a denominational one (guardrail 10). Some applications by analogy are reasonable; say so

### 8. Eucharist, baptism and church practice

- **What it is:** Lines that assume a view of what happens in communion or baptism (real presence, memorial, covenant sign)
- **Label:** **Contested across traditions.** Name the view the line assumes; do not rank it

## Items to Source Before Use

The following need a primary or scholarly citation before they are used in a report. Mark them `[needs source]` if you cannot open one:

- Confession-by-confession section numbers (for example Westminster, Augsburg, Belgic) for each view above
- Page numbers for Aulén, Dodd, Morris or any other secondary work
- Any claim about what a named tradition "has always taught"

## Report Wording Pattern

> "Verse 2, line 3 assumes a penal-substitution view of the cross. That view is widely held in Reformed and evangelical churches. Other traditions emphasize victory over the powers (Colossians 2:15). **Contested across traditions; not unsupported by the text.**"
